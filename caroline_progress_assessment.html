The Meeting Paradox

Meetings are supposed to help companies run more smoothly. Yet for many professionals, especially those working in multinational corporations, back-to-back meetings have become one of the biggest barriers to actually getting work done. Research from Microsoft suggests that the average knowledge worker spends over 57% of their working time in meetings or on email — leaving fewer than half of working hours for focused, independent work.

The problem is structural. In large organisations — whether a public limited company with shareholders to satisfy or a fast-growing start-up trying to stay aligned across teams — the number of meetings tends to increase as companies scale. What begins as a daily standup becomes a weekly sync, which becomes a monthly review, which gradually fills every available hour in the calendar.

Some professionals have started pushing back. A growing number of workers — particularly in marketing, product, and strategy roles — have started keeping their calendars lean by design, passing on meetings where their attendance is not essential and contributing asynchronously instead. They argue that a well-written document or a recorded update can often suffice where a live meeting would eat into the same time budget without producing better outcomes.

Managers, however, are not always convinced. From a leadership perspective, presence signals commitment. Particularly in family businesses or organisations with more traditional hierarchies, being seen to attend — and being visible in decision-making conversations — can still carry as much weight as the quality of one's actual output. The tension between visibility and productivity may be one of the defining workplace challenges of the decade.
